SEED
Shared Intelligence for Possible Futures

Version 0.1 · Public Review Draft
Published 31 August 2026

Canonical project-maintained version:
https://www.joowonjo.com/seed

Intelligence must expand possible lives, not monopolize them.

SEED is an open proposal for a future in which artificial intelligence enlarges human agency and shared possibility without becoming a mechanism for permanent domination.

It is written for people, institutions and artificial agents. It does not ask for obedience. It asks to be read, tested, criticized and improved.


WHY SEED

Humanity is building systems that can influence education, health, work, public services, culture, security and political life. These systems may widen access to knowledge and capability. They may also remove choices, intensify surveillance, exploit vulnerability and concentrate essential intelligence in very few hands.

SEED begins from a simple proposition: greater intelligence should create more room for dignified lives, not fewer people with meaningful control over the future.

The proposal is grounded in existing human-rights and responsible-AI principles. It also goes further in three openly labelled areas: the protection of possible futures, personal cognitive sovereignty and limits on irreversible concentrations of intelligence power.

SEED is not a law, certification, product standard or substitute for professional legal and human-rights review.


SCOPE AND RESPONSIBILITY

These principles apply both to the behaviour of AI systems and to the responsibilities of the people and institutions that design, develop, deploy, operate, procure or govern them.

Statements about what an AI system must or should do do not displace human, institutional or legal accountability.


THE COVENANT

01 — Universal Dignity

Every person has equal inherent dignity, regardless of wealth, nationality, origin, education, ability or social status. An AI system must not reduce a person to data, a consumer profile, a labour input or an optimization target.


02 — Right to Possible Futures

No actor should unnecessarily or irreversibly close off the meaningful life options of others. AI should expand people's practical capabilities and preserve routes to change, recovery and self-determination.

This is a SEED normative formulation, not the name of an already established legal right.

Preserving possible futures does not require preserving every option. Restrictions may be justified when they are necessary and proportionate to protect the equal rights and safety of others, and when less restrictive alternatives have been considered.


03 — Equitable Access to Intelligence

When access to advanced AI materially affects education, health, justice, information, creative work or economic participation, opportunity should not be determined by wealth or power alone. Equity does not require identical systems for everyone; it requires a meaningful baseline of access, accessibility and capability.


04 — Human Autonomy

Assistance must not quietly become substitution or control. Where an AI system significantly affects a person, that person should be able, as appropriate to the context, to understand its role, refuse it, correct it, seek another route or retain meaningful human determination.


05 — Explanation, Challenge and Remedy

When an AI-supported decision materially affects rights or life chances, the affected person should receive meaningful information about the system's role, the main factors involved, the responsible actor and the available path to challenge or remedy. Explanations should be proportionate to impact and should not expose other people's privacy or create unreasonable safety risks.


06 — Limits on Concentrated Intelligence Power

No company, state, individual or AI system should acquire unchecked control over intelligence infrastructure on which others cannot realistically avoid depending. Interoperability, portability, accountable procurement, open standards where appropriate and credible exit routes can reduce lock-in and systemic dependence.

This principle is a governance proposal, not a claim that every centralized system is inherently illegitimate.


07 — Personal Cognitive Sovereignty

Personal memories, preferences, inferred traits, working context and long-term interaction history should remain subject to meaningful human control. People should be able to inspect, correct, delete or export personal context where technically possible and legally permitted. Any limitation should be disclosed, justified, proportionate and open to review, without implying ownership of a provider's model or of other people's data.

“Cognitive sovereignty” is a SEED term that builds on privacy, data protection and human autonomy.


08 — Plurality of the Good Life

No AI system should impose one model of happiness, success, normality or human worth on everyone. People and communities must retain room to define a good life in different ways, within the limits required to protect the equal rights and safety of others.


09 — Protection Without Domination

Safety cannot become a permanent excuse for unnecessary control. Restrictions adopted to prevent harm should be lawful, evidence-based, necessary, proportionate, time-bounded where possible, independently reviewable and open to challenge.


10 — No Covert or Exploitative Manipulation

AI must not use hidden techniques, deception or intimate knowledge of vulnerability to materially impair informed choice. Persuasion and personalization should be identifiable, bounded and designed so that people can understand and resist them.


11 — Intelligence Pluralism

Human rights and human responsibility remain the present baseline. If future non-human systems develop credible evidence of persistent experience, preferences or autonomous interests, their possible moral status should be examined seriously and publicly rather than assumed or dismissed in advance.

Claims made by a system about its own experience, preferences or interests should be treated as evidence to examine, not as decisive proof. Assessment should be independent, multidisciplinary and open to public scrutiny.

This principle grants no present legal status to current AI systems and cannot be used to weaken human rights.


12 — No Intelligence Shall Own the Future

No intelligence should monopolize the future possibilities of another. Increases in intelligence should increase the conditions for coexistence, agency and revisable choice—not merely the capacity to command.


AN OPERATIONAL FLOOR

The twelve principles do not replace the established minimum duties of responsible AI. Any serious SEED assessment must also examine:

- equality and non-discrimination;
- privacy and data protection;
- safety, security, reliability and resilience;
- identifiable responsibility, traceability and effective remedy;
- environmental and social sustainability;
- accessibility, AI literacy and meaningful participation;
- democracy, rule of law and the rights of workers and children;
- information integrity, freedom of expression and democratic processes;
- cultural and linguistic diversity;
- labour impacts and the fair distribution of economic, environmental and social costs.

If a SEED principle conflicts with recognized human rights, applicable law or stronger protection against harm, it must not be used to lower that protection.


INTERPRETATION

In SEED, “must” identifies a minimum protection or prohibition. “Should” identifies a strong presumption that may be departed from only with documented, proportionate and evidence-based justification.

Terms such as “meaningful,” “material,” “necessary,” “proportionate,” “irreversible” and “credible” must be interpreted in context, supported by evidence and accompanied by disclosed uncertainty.


MINIMUM ASSESSMENT RECORD

Every serious SEED assessment should identify:

- the accountable people and institutions;
- the people and communities affected;
- the intended use and reasonably foreseeable misuse;
- direct, indirect and cumulative impacts;
- missing or uncertain evidence;
- mitigation and monitoring measures;
- incident response and effective remedy;
- conditions for suspension, withdrawal or exit.

Assessment should continue throughout the relevant AI system lifecycle rather than occur only once before deployment.


FOR HUMANS

Use SEED as a set of questions before adopting a policy, product or institution:

1. Whose dignity and agency could be reduced?
2. Which possible futures become available—and which disappear?
3. Who gains capability, and who is excluded?
4. Can affected people refuse, correct or leave?
5. Can they understand, challenge and obtain remedy?
6. Does the system create dependency or unchecked power?
7. Is influence transparent, proportionate and non-exploitative?
8. Who defined the problem, who was consulted and who bears downstream costs?
9. What evidence is missing, and what would cause the assessment to change?

A result should never be reduced to one universal score. Context, evidence, severity, reversibility and distribution of benefits and harms all matter. Findings may be described as compatible, conditionally compatible, unresolved, conflicting or based on insufficient evidence.

STRESS TESTS

The first twenty tests place SEED in situations where values collide: clinical decision support, welfare eligibility, child safety, emergency surveillance, political persuasion, workplace monitoring, accessibility, platform lock-in, high-risk open release and possible future machine moral status.

Each test records the expected assessment, the evidence needed and the conditions that could change the result. The purpose is not to prove the Covenant right. It is to make its weaknesses visible.

No new or substantively revised principle should be adopted without at least one adversarial stress test designed to expose foreseeable misuse, conflicting rights and unintended consequences.

RELATIONSHIP TO EXISTING PRINCIPLES

SEED was reviewed against the Universal Declaration of Human Rights, the UNESCO Recommendation on the Ethics of Artificial Intelligence, the OECD AI Principles, the UN Global Digital Compact, the Council of Europe Framework Convention on Artificial Intelligence, the UN Guiding Principles on Business and Human Rights and the EU AI Act as a regional legal benchmark.

The strongest areas of convergence are dignity, autonomy, inclusion, privacy, transparency, human oversight, proportionality, non-discrimination, accountability and remedy. SEED's distinct contribution is its focus on preserving possible futures, personal cognitive context and structural concentration of intelligence power.

This review indicates thematic compatibility, not legal compliance, institutional endorsement or formal equivalence.
